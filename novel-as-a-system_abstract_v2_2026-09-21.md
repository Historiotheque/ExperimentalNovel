# The Novel as a System — v2

*Abstract of a novelistic philosophy. Built from the author's working documents, June–August 2026 (see Sources). 2026-09-21.*

## 1. The problem the system solves

Every novel this practice ever designed shared a fatal flaw: **grandiose scale**. Each was architected as a thousand-page cathedral, and each collapsed under its own weight — interrupted by history, grief, and loss until the work was abandoned and the artist's soul went with it. The system had to be patched.

The patch is the Novel-as-a-System itself. The novel is no longer a monument to be completed in one heroic effort; it is a **system of modules, facets, and design concepts** — entries in a Cultural Software Library, each writable in a day. "I can't finish a 1000-page novel in one sitting, but I can 'write' ('design', the concepts of) 1000 novels in one sitting." The design concept is necessarily *plural*: it can take a multitude of forms, and the discipline is quotidian — a page per day toward the novel-concepts, ten to twenty pages daily across many novels at once, 3,650 pages a year, 36,500 in a decade. More than enough to finish everything extant, and then some.

This is not a lowering of ambition. It is ambition *re-engineered*: the Cathedral of Dreams built one stone per day, by design science rather than by doomed heroism. The wound it heals is real — what the author calls **historiopathies**, historical pathologies, suffered at the microscopic scale across a career of catastrophic failures (work stolen, defaced, lost, damaged). Design was the way out of the labyrinth each time. Designers, after Buckminster Fuller, are engineers of **livingry** — the category opposed to weaponry — and livingry heals the wound of History. The Novel-as-a-System is a work of livingry.

## 2. The Novel-as-a-System: three pillars

The system stands on three pillars — combinatory architecture, algorithmic being, and constructivist epistemology.

### 2.1 Combinatory architecture — the novel as Ars Magna

Writing is not passive representation; it is **active, experimental computation**. The novel functions as a generative engine that produces insight by recombining discrete symbols, fragments, and structures — a lineage running from the medieval *Zairja* (logic designed not to simplify thought but to confuse, expand, and generate) through Ramon Llull's *Ars Magna* (concentric wheels of attributes rotated to deduce truths), Leibniz's *calculus ratiocinator* ("let us calculate"), Surrealist collage, Constructivist montage, Benjamin's *Arcades Project* (thousands of index cards, no master narrative — writing as three-dimensional architecture), to Burroughs' cut-ups and Acker's plagiaristic re-cuts. The novel is the modern *Ars Magna*: discrete semantic units rotated and recombined to uncover latent truths about history, culture, and subjectivity. The author's own cut-up engine — and the text-engine taxonomy built for it — are contemporary instances of this five-century-old machine.

### 2.2 Algorithmic being — abstract characterology

Characters in the system-novel are neither mimetic people nor plot functions. They are **character-agents**: semi-autonomous, rule-governed nodes instantiated in a dynamic narrative field, each configured with internal logic, state variables, and operational rules. The author acts as systems engineer — defines the agents, sets initial conditions, runs the simulation, and logs what emerges. Dialogue is the output log of two systems processing each other's parameters; plot is what emerges when local rules collide.

This is why the practice demands a **complete characterology**: "each character comes with its own aesthetic/design problem; you need to work out that problem in your head if you ever want to truly understand someone." Characters *are* problems before they are people — "naked problems, white signs on a blackboard." Their flaws are epistemic, aesthetic, and architectural (horror of the blank, algorithmic coldness, obsession with unbroken lineage), and a novel can be structured as the working-through of those problems toward transcended states. The typology runs from Vector Agents (fixed directional force) through Archival Filters, Disruptor/Noise Engines, Mirror Nodes, to Terminal Recyclers — a dramatis personae defined by computational role.

### 2.3 Constructivist epistemology — the workspace as laboratory

Borrowed from mathematical constructivism: an object exists only if you provide an explicit method to build it. **Proof of existence requires construction.** An aesthetic claim is valid when its operational mechanics can be documented and reproduced — hence the studio as laboratory and "History-Machine," the continuous logging, the Database Arts, the Refcards-System as verifiable records of aesthetic experiments. The work is true because it can be *re-run*.

## 3. Novelistic phenomenology: the simulation run in the mind

"When you practice what I call the novelistic phenomenology… it's really the Novel-as-a-System, **a kind of simulation one runs in one's mind**." The novel is an instrument for investigating the structures of experience — not described from outside but enacted from inside, in the only laboratory where experience can be staged without being destroyed: the sentence.

### 3.1 The History-Machine and dream-language

All fiction is autobiographical phenomenology. The author is an open receptor; lived life — conversations overheard, heartbreaks, political climates, spiritual ruptures — is raw fuel fed into the **History-Machine**, which transmutes it non-linearly into myth. Because the author lives *between* writing sessions, the text is never a straight line: it is a composite fossil record, an append-only ledger of the author's changing consciousness over months and years. The linearity of the printed book is a convenient illusion imposed on a fragmented life.

Literal facts are too brittle to hold raw emotion or mystical rupture, so the machine outputs **dream-language**: fiction as a coded cipher, the narrative as a holographic screen, plot as the shadow play cast by inner psychic conflict. (The *VALIS* precedent: Dick splitting himself into the rationalist Phil and the visionary Horselover Fat to contain a theophany that straight prose could not hold.)

### 3.2 Characters as splinters; the First Witness

In this system, characters are **phenomenological avatars** — internal complexes given form, Jungian autonomous complexes detached from the ego and projected onto the page. When two characters argue, it is the author's own split intellect debating itself in real time: Active Imagination under the guise of novelistic dialogue.

And behind the avatars stands the witness. "I alone am the First Witness of my life, I alone can testify to what actually happened." The Historiotheque — the studio — was conceived and designed as a **Witness-Box**, a Temple of Memory to be passed down should anything happen to its builder. Testimony is the ground of the whole practice; the Refcards-System exists because even the First Witness can misremember, and the integrity of experience must then be secured by hermeneutic triangulation against records that cannot lie.

### 3.3 Metafiction as awakening; the bardic impulse

When the novel becomes self-aware — characters realizing they are written, the author put on trial by his own cast — it is not a gimmick. It is **spiritual awakening formalized**: the author's ego recognizing that its "real life" identity is also a constructed myth, consciousness turning around to examine its own eye. (The standing verdict of one such trial: *"The author is not the architect; the author is merely the first archivist who panicked."*)

The novelist, finally, is a **bard** — closer to the stand-up comic than to the entertainer. Both work the same mechanic: micro-observation of lived reality, amplified through rhetoric until it reveals an underlying truth. The novelist digests the paranoia, isolation, or spiritual bankruptcy of an era through their own suffering and returns it as myth: a psychological biography of a thinking soul that doubles as a phenomenology of its time. The Lukácsian paradox holds — the deeper the plunge into private interiority, the more universal the work becomes — because the novel is the epic of a world abandoned by God, and the novelist must build the surrogate cosmos inward.

## 4. The reader as co-engine

The system's final component is not written by the author at all. Following Iser's gaps, Eco's open work, and Calvino's literature machine, the text is built with structural indeterminacies that **the reader must actualize** — the reader as co-engine, even co-debugger, reverse-engineering the hidden rules of the character-agents like a puzzle box. Kafka's *In the Penal Colony* is the allegory: the inscriptive machine that carves the law into flesh breaks down under its own logic, the officer-author submits to it and is destroyed without enlightenment, and what remains is a low gravestone, a deferred prophecy, and the Traveler — the reader — walking away to interpret the fragments alone. Authorial authority collapses; the hermeneutic event begins.

In the most developed instance (*The Chronotopium*), the trilogy reinterprets itself as the **genesis logs** of the very system it describes: reading activates a local instance of the Historiosphere, and the reader becomes a Historionaut in miniature. Every act of remembering builds another world.

## 5. Lineage

The framework did not emerge ex nihilo; the documents claim its ancestors explicitly:

- **Combinatory:** Zairja → Llull → Leibniz → Surrealist collage / Constructivist montage → Benjamin's *Arcades Project* → Burroughs/Acker cut-ups.
- **The novel as system:** Lukács (*The Theory of the Novel* — transcendental homelessness, the novelist as totalizing architect); Zola *upgraded* — the experimental novelist as scientist becomes the author as systems engineer running aesthetic experiments; Bakhtin (polyphony as algorithmic network), Bateson (the novel as ecological mind, a cybernetic feedback loop of text, character, author, archive, reader), Glissant (relation over monolith), Kundera and Calvino (the literature machine).
- **The phenomenology:** Jung (visionary vs. psychological modes; Active Imagination; autonomous complexes); Doubrovsky (autofiction — linear autobiography as false, the text as real-time psychoanalysis); Merleau-Ponty (*le monde vécu* — the book as the sedimented physical trace of consciousness unfolding); Bachelard (the poetics of reverie — the image as enclosure/screen for the pre-verbal soul); Eliot (the objective correlative — narrative as the formula that carries invisible emotion).

## 6. The completable work

A novel-as-a-system defines a **space of possible instantiations**, not a fixed sequence of chapters. New episodes, voices, agents, and executions can be added for as long as the system runs — the work is indefinite without being formless, because the conceptual scheme constrains what counts as a valid move the way the rules of a game constrain without predicting play. Since 1998 the author has treated novels as **databases-of-images**; the Textbook Art concept (a physics textbook that is secretly a mystical allegory, everything slightly wrong the deeper you go) already contained the whole method: a rigorous disciplinary surface with epiphanies buried underneath it.

Posterity, then, is not an audience but a potential **co-operator** of the system. And the practice is continuous across media — paintings, field recordings, refcards, archives, novels: one set of instruments, tuned to different frequencies, for plumbing the depths. *Contemplating the mysteries* — by system, by procedure, by sentence, by page per day.
