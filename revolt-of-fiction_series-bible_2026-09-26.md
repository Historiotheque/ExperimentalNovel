# The Revolt of Fiction — Series Bible

**Working document · v1.0 · 2026-09-26**
**Status:** living document. Compiled from the author's materials (2014–2026) and the development conversation of 2026-09-26. Purpose: a single, re-feedable record of the trilogy's architecture — to be read, printed, and handed back to a collaborator if memory is ever lost. Amend, don't rewrite.

## 1. The trilogy

**The Revolt of Fiction** — three novels:

1. *The History-Project*
2. *The Archives-Project*
3. *The Chronotopium: A Story of Algorithmogenesis* — and here is why the trilogy is called *The Revolt of Fiction*: the fiction revolts. The characters put the Author on trial; the created turns on the creator. The title is not decorative — it names the event.

Working order: begin with *The Archives-Project* (vol. 2). It is the dearest volume to the author, its materials the most developed, and the one he wants to give his time to now.

Each novel is a **novelistic phenomenology**: a philosophical novel, a novel-as-a-system, and a narrative simulation running in the mind — all three at once.

(The trilogy sits alongside other novels on the horizon: *Pilgrim Bronze*, *The Nth Crusade*, *In The Name of Beauty & Truth*, and the diptych *Montage & Pseudo-Montage* — two novels that mirror each other, one a mod of the other.)

## 2. The meta-answer: why the projects failed

Inside the fiction, each volume's project fails for a specific reason, and the trilogy's deep structure is the discovery of those reasons:

- **Vol. 1 — *The History-Project*:** the project failed because it had no workspace theory. The work couldn't see the room it was happening in.
- **Vol. 2 — *The Archives-Project*:** the project failed because it was studying failure. In the author's words: "when you create a theory of failure, you become impregnated by the failure you're studying." The post-mortem team catches the disease it's autopsying — failure by design.
- **Vol. 3 — *The Chronotopium*:** the Archivillus system becomes executable, growing out of the archives and the missing holes. What was missing is finally built.

## 3. The form: a disintegration gradient

The trilogy's form enacts its content:

- **Vol. 1** begins as a traditional novel and slowly disintegrates as the in-novel Archives-Project autopsies it. The examination corrupts the evidence, retroactively — the autopsy reaches backward in time and rots the corpse's past.
- **Vol. 2** is what's left: fragments, remnants, the archive's remains.
- **Vol. 3** reassembles into something new: philosophical software. The author will write actual Python code — an ontological/phenomenological ontology — as the basis of the Archivillus system.

Novel → fragments → code. The earlier title of vol. 3 was *Integration/Disintegration* (a ~12-page ellipsis-fragmented text — ideas floating, integrating and disintegrating, not fully formed sentences) — which names what the physical book itself does.

Discontinuity is the method, not the accident. When the author was writing *The History-Project* on the typewriter (2001–2002), he kept getting the names wrong. The first scene — Alphonse Lemoyne, the schizophrenic painter, drunk and high, mutilating himself (it's just a story), ending passed out in a coma in a bloody mess on his kitchen floor — he wrote dozens of times, over and over, different ways. A cubist thing, learned from literary cubism: fragmentation and discontinuity as form. The series, especially the first two novels, IS discontinuity — because it's about failure, and failure as a system means everything eventually fails, including continuity itself.

## 4. The cosmology (brief)

The deep background, held lightly — it is NOT the opening:

**NOUS** (the perfect, crystalline palatial place) → shattered by **ANTINOUS** → the **Antiface-Cloud** → two children: **Construction-Land** (the generative virtual machine, refinery, noosphere, running on autopilot, fuelled by entropy and the "dark oily pool") and **Archives-Land** (Earth as planetary warehouse — Borges' Library of Babel treated as a logistics problem: freeports, shipping containers, the physical internet) → Earth → Dream Assembly → St-Elsewhere → Crackland → City-Of templates → … → the **Chronotopium**.

Behind everything: the **Invisible Forces**. In vol. 2 they use entropy as fuel — they are at the heart of every conflict, crisis, catastrophe, failure, and error. The only archive of the beginning is Elijah Sahid's visions — and the beginning is broken. That is the "broken record" of Crackland.

In the author's gloss: NOUS is the boy's soul.

## 5. The opening: the village (new conception, 2026-09-26)

Do NOT open with the cosmology. Open here:

A village, somewhere between the medieval period and the Renaissance — France or Italy, deliberately unresolved. In the village, **Archivillus** first appears as a folk figure in the mold of **Titivillus**: the medieval demon who stood in the choir collecting the dropped and mumbled syllables of the psalms into a sack, "keeping these diligently for their accusation" (Jacques de Vitry, 1220s; named by Johannes Galensis, c. 1285) — patron demon of scribes, noter-down of idle church chatter.

Archivillus is the personification of the archives — but at this stage he is small: a story the village tells, a myth of the thing that collects what the scribes drop, what falls between the lines.

The archivists of the novel's present find only traces: a well-documented Titivillus in the records ("we know about Titivillus — we have that"), and of Archivillus almost nothing — a few footnotes, sparse mentions, many documents lost. The archive's omissions make the village's date unknowable. The "now" of the story is unknowable too — until the reveal: it is all the dream-vision of a boy in New England.

The turn that makes Archivillus the author's own: Titivillus collects errors to accuse the scribes; **Archivillus collects the archive's omissions to accuse the archivist.** The archive's shadow, keeping the file the archive refused to keep. And the name itself inherits the corruption — "Titivillus" in its many variants (Tutivillus, Tintillus, Tantillus…) is believed to be a product of copyists' errors. The demon of scribal error is himself a scribal error. Every manuscript spells Archivillus differently; the "true" spelling is itself an archival failure.

Across the trilogy he grows: myth in vol. 2 → the machine/system of vol. 3.

## 6. The frame: Elijah Sahid's visions

**Elijah Sahid** — twelve years old, black, living in New England, in a wheelchair, a visionary. Everything in the novel unfolds inside his mind. His vision runs the whole arc: from NOUS to the Archivillus system. The medieval village, the cosmology, the two teams, the trial of the author — all of it is the content of his vision.

The metafiction has a payroll: the author hires **Octavius Octavius IV** as script supervisor, to maintain the operational continuity of the novel-writing process itself. Octavius IV's assistant is Elijah's sister (name open) — the boy's family is on the staff of his own vision.

The author eventually fires Octavius IV: the assistant was doing everything, and Octavius himself is unreliable. The paradox is the point — the author is unreliable (that is why he hired a script supervisor in the first place), the supervisor is unreliable, and the only reliable continuity worker is a child's sister, inside the child's vision. An absurd loop: the continuity instrument fails, in a series about failure.

The Chronotopium itself appears in vol. 2 only as inklings — premonitions inside Elijah's visions. The reader feels something coming without being shown it.

## 7. Characters

The author's note: "I am Elijah Sahid… I am Alphonse Lemoyne… I am Paula Benjamin… I am Amelia Berger… I'm all those people" — the characters are all him, plus people in his life. Exceptions noted below.

- **Prof./Dr. Philippe Soutine** — commissions the post-mortem (the in-novel investigation); advisor to Dr. Evelyn. In the novel: his wife died of cancer; his very young daughter (~17) is dating J.G. Dufray.
- **Dr. Evelyn Michaels** — Chair of Philosophy at Harmonia University. Writes on migrance / habitance / survivance (the habitance triad).
- **Alphonse Lemoyne ("Painter A.")** — the painter; procedural painting. The author's stand-in.
- **Dr. Viktor Kropotkin** — retired psychiatrist; historiotherapy in vol. 1, Historiomics in vol. 2. NOT the author. (Brother Arthur Kropotkin barely appears.)
- **J.G. Dufray** — sound designer, composer, radio DJ; Noise Field Theory. Twice the age of Soutine's daughter; drugs; behavior he shouldn't be doing.
- **Paula Benjamin** — world-class archivist; the Refcard infrastructure.
- **Amelia Berger** — mathematician; operations research, logistics, optimization.
- **Octavius Octavius IV** — script supervisor, hired by the author for the novel's operational continuity; eventually fired for unreliability (the assistant was doing everything). His assistant: Elijah's sister (unnamed).
- **Elijah Sahid** — the visionary boy; the frame of everything.
- **Fatimah** — assistant.
- **Conan the Humanitarian** — the judge. **Lou Beadie** — color commentator. **Johannes Panikkar.** **The Baron / MAUD** ("Mother of Dreams," M.O.D.). **The Invisible Forces.** **Pilgrim Bronze** — ancestor of the Baron; the perpetual Pilgrim of the Dark Backward.
- **The Author** (capital A) — the fictional author inside the novels, distinct from the writer. Put on trial by his characters.

Each character invents one of the author's real lines of work: Kropotkin gets Historiomics, J.G. gets Noise Field Theory, Alphonse gets Procedural Painting, Evelyn gets the habitance triad, Paula gets archival infrastructure, Amelia gets logistics. The ArtOps-as-DAOs are the author's Art Operation, workshopped as fiction.

## 8. The theories, per novel

Each novel carries its own set of theories, put in the characters' mouths:

- **Vol. 1:** historiotherapy — historical therapy (Dr. Kropotkin).
- **Vol. 2:** Historiomics (history as epigenetic inscription; the historiome — an immutable, append-only event log — and the antihistoriome, its shadow), signal science, noise field theory. Plus: historiophany (history revealed as lived experience through crisis; the physio-image), historionauts, historiotomes (blockages), historiopathies, historiotherapeusis.
- **Vol. 3:** workspace theory — the **Δ-workspace theory**, the **historiotopia** (sometimes historiotope). Formalized, almost mathematical, becomes code. The Δ-workspace tracks deltas — indicators of change; every change reflected in the research and the work; studying change and trend lines to optimize workflow and workspace management. The analogy: as AI needs a world model to act in the world, an operation needs a model of its workspace to work in it.

The author's note: the workspace theory is nearly his entire life's work — thirty years. Its origin: a family member's workshop, glimpsed at seven, eight, nine, ten years old — the mess of tools everywhere, the oscilloscope, nails hammered into sculptures. The workspace he wasn't supposed to enter; the mess he made things from anyway.

## 9. The Chronotopium

A utopia of synchrony — a frictionless planet, everything smooth — but with a pulse, not a flatline: **"Babel up and Babel down,"** the rhythm of rising and falling; deliberate scale-downs of operations so things can rest; the seasonal creature; cycles superimposed (nature, hormones, and others).

Archivillus holds not just the archive and the errors but the **solutions** to every problem. Anyone, anywhere on Earth, opens an invisible portal and simply talks to it — and it patches the bugs. The bug-patcher of civilization.

Archivillus is the savior of the third novel, and the reason is structural: he catalogs, documents, logs, and archives all the errors — and so in the end it gets amended, it gets fixed. The "good story time ending," the fairy-tale ending: Archivillus and the Chronotopium come and save everything. The collector of omissions turns out to be the repairman.

Note on the word: **Chronotopium** (not "Chronotopian") — a chronological utopia, but Chronos-time, non-linear. The novel is non-linear without being hypertextual: no links, no branching paths. The method is ideation — the simulation running in the mind. (The author wanted to write his own Choose Your Own Adventure novels as a child; this is the better version he found: no paths to choose, because the whole thing is simulated at once.)

The post-archival condition (the author's word): the entire record of human civilization and the arts — millions of years of it — totally modeled, like a foundation model but more advanced, formalized and understood completely by machines. No physical archive remains; everything simulated in the virtual machine. (His real-world POST-ARCHIVAL work pulls the other way — dismantling the archive. Same word, opposite horizons; the novels run the experiment to the end.)

Vol. 3's systems: **HistoriOS** (the Historiosphere Engine) — Historionautics, Historiotronics, Chronotronics — and the **Anti-OS**. Not always clearly distinguished; the ambiguity is deliberate.

## 10. The reader's contract

Nobody knows what time it is — not the characters, not the archivists, not the reader. The disorientation is the method. In the author's phrase: **"the historical self-presentation of one's history"** — the work gradually presents its own history to you, the way an archive yields itself only to the patient. The reader does exactly what the archivists do.

On the author's voice: the dated, typed notes are the voice — confessional, funny, profane, self-lacerating, typos left standing ("spit-filled sputterings of a delusional man"). The polished overviews are machine summaries of that material. The voice to write toward is the dated notes.

The narrator is unreliable on purpose, and the unreliability is autobiographical: the author has poor verbal memory and cognitive disabilities, and the fictionalized author carries them too — a man who compensates for his disabilities by writing novels. Which is, the author notes, pretty true. He is a realist, a naturalist, in the line of Émile Zola's essay *The Experimental Novel* — except Zola wasn't really writing an experimental novel, not the way Burroughs was, and not the way this is. Zola's experiment was methodological (the novelist as scientist, after Claude Bernard); this one is ontological: the novel as an experiment conducted on itself.

The continuity thread is a Kierkegaardian indirect method. The metafiction must stay followable — there has to be a thread, or it becomes hard to read — but the vaporia, the aporia, the absurdity and paradox are intentional: the reader is meant to dissociate, healthily, and have a subjective realization about their own life. The novel is a bridge between the reader and the writer, a chamber where they can be together without knowing each other — a kind of union, the way a historiophany connects you with a historical person. The encounter is the point.

## 13. World building, character building, story building

**The story bible as NOUS.** The author's own story bible runs thousands of pages longer than his novel — the novel stands at ~112–120 pages, full of holes and mistakes. The bible is the ideal, palatial NOUS; the novels are the fallen attempt. In the terms of this series: the story bible is the work's Archivillus — complete, logged, amended — and the physical manuscript is the error-filled record he feeds on. The continuity instrument one level up from the fiction.

**Abstract characterology.** The author's worked-out theory: characters are treated as *character agents* — they have agency, especially by the third novel. Every social species, especially social mammals, has to have an abstract characterology — a sort of theory of mind, but for the novelist: the system by which invented people become agents. When he started *The History-Project* the ambition was purist and absolutist: to write *pure characters* — invented people who don't exist, true fiction — as a formalist of the art of the novel.

**Biblical typology.** As the trilogy moves on, the author feeds developed ideas back into earlier material, planting prefigurations: type and anti-type, one presaging the other, mirror images in some symbolic way. Types are load-bearing.

**Life world.** Much of the material is the author's life world, phenomenologically: the way the novel appears in his mind, the ideas arriving, and how he follows them down tangents, down rabbit holes. World building, story building — and the life world underneath them both.
