**Working document · v1.1 · 2026-10-18**
**Status:** living document. This version supersedes v1.0 (2026-09-26), which remains in the repository as its own file — the versions of this bible accumulate as dated files. V1.0 was compiled from my materials (2014–2026) and the development conversation of 2026-09-26; v1.1 carries that text forward with the amendments and clarifications of the audit of the sent novel documents and my rulings of 2026-10-17. One correction is global: v1.0 spoke of "the author" in the third person. That was an error. This bible is written in my first person. Purpose, unchanged: a single, re-feedable record of the trilogy's architecture — to be read, printed, and handed back to a collaborator if memory is ever lost. Amend, don't rewrite.

## 1. The trilogy

**The Revolt of Fiction** — three novels:

1. *The History-Project*
2. *The Archives-Project*
3. *The Chronotopium: A Story of Algorithmogenesis* — and here is why the trilogy is called *The Revolt of Fiction*: the fiction revolts. The characters put the Author on trial; the created turns on the creator. The title is not decorative — it names the event.

Working order: begin with *The Archives-Project* (vol. 2). It is the dearest volume to me, its materials the most developed, and the one I want to give my time to now.

Each novel is a **novelistic phenomenology**: a philosophical novel, a novel-as-a-system, and a narrative simulation running in the mind — all three at once.

**The overlaps.** The trilogy was never written in sequence. Much of the Chronotopium and Archivillus material started while I was still working technically on *The Archives-Project*, and the same holds for the first two volumes: each novel begins inside the previous one's working period. Historiography is a really important part of the end of *The Archives-Project* for this reason. It is a trilogy, but one that asks what a trilogy is and what its limits are; *Integration/Disintegration* belongs to that question.

**The dimensional genesis.** The trilogy ascends in dimensions as it generates: *The History-Project* is **historiogenesis** — a one-dimensional story, maybe two. *The Archives-Project* is **archivogenesis** — the historiogenesis taking on another dimension: maybe two, maybe three. *The Chronotopium* is **algorithmogenesis** — the multi-dimensional one: three-dimensional, and four — across dimensions. The counts are as I state them; the ascent is the doctrine.

(The trilogy sits alongside other novels on the horizon: *Pilgrim Bronze*, *The Nth Crusade*, *In The Name of Beauty & Truth*, and the diptych *Montage & Pseudo-Montage* — two novels that mirror each other, one a mod of the other.)

## 2. The meta-answer: why the projects failed

Inside the fiction, each volume's project fails for a specific reason, and the trilogy's deep structure is the discovery of those reasons. The failures ascend:

- **Vol. 1 — *The History-Project*:** the project failed because it had no workspace theory. The work couldn't see the room it was happening in. **Failure by lack.** The period document already delivers the verdict, in my own hand, at the volume's end: "The History-Project was a complete failure in the end."
- **Vol. 2 — *The Archives-Project*:** the project failed because it was studying failure. In my words: "when you create a theory of failure, you become impregnated by the failure you're studying." The post-mortem team catches the disease it's autopsying — failure by design. **Failure by collapse.**
- **Vol. 3 — *The Chronotopium*:** the Archivillus system becomes executable, growing out of the archives and the missing holes. What was missing is finally built. **And that is the catastrophe — failure by completion** (see §9): a chronological utopia of perfect synchrony removes the risks of rejection and failure, and so removes the freedom of choice — and where freedom is gone, authentic love is impossible. The third volume does not fail for want of anything. It fails because it succeeds.

## 3. The form: a disintegration gradient

The trilogy's form enacts its content:

- **Vol. 1** begins as a traditional novel and slowly disintegrates as the in-novel Archives-Project autopsies it. The examination corrupts the evidence, retroactively — the autopsy reaches backward in time and rots the corpse's past.
- **Vol. 2** is what's left: fragments, remnants, the archive's remains.
- **Vol. 3** reassembles into something new: philosophical software. I will write actual Python code — an ontological/phenomenological ontology — as the basis of the Archivillus system.

Novel → fragments → code. The earlier title of vol. 3 was *Integration/Disintegration* (a ~12-page ellipsis-fragmented text — ideas floating, integrating and disintegrating, not fully formed sentences) — which names what the physical book itself does. The dimensional genesis of §1 is this gradient read upward: as the books disintegrate in form, they ascend in dimension.

Discontinuity is the method, not the accident. When I was writing *The History-Project* on the typewriter (2001–2002), I kept getting the names wrong. The first scene — Alphonse Lemoyne, the schizophrenic painter, drunk and high, mutilating himself (it's just a story), ending passed out in a coma in a bloody mess on his kitchen floor — I wrote dozens of times, over and over, different ways. A cubist thing, learned from literary cubism: fragmentation and discontinuity as form. The series, especially the first two novels, IS discontinuity — because it's about failure, and failure as a system means everything eventually fails, including continuity itself. The name-instability of the period is not a defect in the record; it is part of my methodology, and versioning and revision control are how the canon settles (§7).

## 4. The cosmology

Each novel runs on two layers: the story of its fictional project, with its characters — and its own mythical, cosmological layer. V1.0 blurred the layers into one chain; this version states them apart. There is a shared spine, and each novel has its layer of its own.

**The shared spine.** **NOUS** (the perfect, crystalline palatial place) → shattered by **ANTINOUS** → the **Antiface-Cloud** → two children: **Construction-Land** (the generative virtual machine, refinery, noosphere, running on autopilot, fuelled by entropy and the "dark oily pool") and **Archives-Land** (Earth as planetary warehouse — Borges' Library of Babel treated as a logistics problem: freeports, shipping containers, the physical internet) → Earth → and from there the layers separate, each novel carrying its layer of its own. Behind everything: the **Invisible Forces**. In vol. 2 they use entropy as fuel — they are at the heart of every conflict, crisis, catastrophe, failure, and error. The only archive of the beginning is Elijah Sahid's visions — and the beginning is broken. That is the "broken record" of Crackland.

In my gloss: NOUS is the boy's soul.

**Vol. 1's layer** is Crackland, St-Elsewhere, the Dream Assembly, and the Baron (of Saint-Laurent, of Saint-Elsewhere). The period cosmology, as the 2003–2004 documents have it, runs: Elsewhere → Dream Assembly → Crackland → City-Of → **the Room with White Walls**. NOUS and ANTINOUS do not occur in vol. 1's period stratum at all — they belong to vol. 2's layer, and the audit confirms the separation. The only titled Baron in the period text is the **Baron of St-Hilaire**. Crackland itself is not a separate novel: it was written in the same years as *The History-Project* (2000–2004), the two essentially fused in one master work, and it stands in this bible as vol. 1's annex document (see §13).

**Vol. 2's layer** is NOUS at the beginning of time, the Antiface-Cloud, ANTINOUS — the cosmology proper, the source.

**Vol. 3's layer** was never much written, and I am developing it now — world building. Its cosmological model as I conceive it: waves, signals — **versions**. Many versions, coexisting and conflicting with one another. A multi-dimensional novel because its world is multi-dimensional. Phase-locking every version into one is the cosmological form of the fatal flaw (§9): freedom lives in the interference.

**The generation of characters.** The cosmology is not only a backdrop; it generates the cast. The concrete characters flow out of a source — the source where NOUS is. At the source stand the archetypes: from NOUS come the Invisible Forces first, then Maud, the Baron, the priest of St-Elsewhere, the Messenger Boy. Flowing outward, beings become progressively more concrete and specific — Alphonse Lemoyne, Evelyn, Kropotkin — with more specificities and technical characteristics. The source continually generates new characters; the cast is not closed. This generation system is the Abstract Characterology inside the novelistic phenomenology (§13): near the source, beings are modular, hold offices, change names; downstream, forms settle. The rulings of §7 sit on this gradient.

## 5. The opening: the village (new conception, 2026-09-26)

Do NOT open with the cosmology. Open here:

A village, somewhere between the medieval period and the Renaissance — France or Italy, deliberately unresolved. In the village, **Archivillus** first appears as a folk figure in the mold of **Titivillus**: the medieval demon who stood in the choir collecting the dropped and mumbled syllables of the psalms into a sack, "keeping these diligently for their accusation" (Jacques de Vitry, 1220s; named by Johannes Galensis, c. 1285) — patron demon of scribes, noter-down of idle church chatter.

Archivillus is the personification of the archives — but at this stage he is small: a story the village tells, a myth of the thing that collects what the scribes drop, what falls between the lines.

The archivists of the novel's present find only traces: a well-documented Titivillus in the records ("we know about Titivillus — we have that"), and of Archivillus almost nothing — a few footnotes, sparse mentions, many documents lost. The archive's omissions make the village's date unknowable. The "now" of the story is unknowable too — until the reveal: it is all the dream-vision of a boy in New England.

The turn that makes Archivillus my own: Titivillus collects errors to accuse the scribes; **Archivillus collects the archive's omissions to accuse the archivist.** The archive's shadow, keeping the file the archive refused to keep. And the name itself inherits the corruption — "Titivillus" in its many variants (Tutivillus, Tintillus, Tantillus…) is believed to be a product of copyists' errors. The demon of scribal error is himself a scribal error. Every manuscript spells Archivillus differently; the "true" spelling is itself an archival failure.

Across the trilogy he grows: myth in vol. 2 → the machine/system of vol. 3.

## 6. The frame: Elijah Sahid's visions

**Elijah Sahid** — twelve years old, black, living in New England, in a wheelchair, a visionary. Everything in the novel unfolds inside his mind. His vision runs the whole arc: from NOUS to the Archivillus system. The medieval village, the cosmology, the two teams, the trial of the author — all of it is the content of his vision.

(A provenance note, from the audit: Elijah — and Fatimah — do not occur anywhere in vol. 1's period stratum, the 2003–2004 documents. The frame is a later development of the work, not a period element. I state it here so the bible does not back-project it.)

The metafiction has a payroll: I hire **Octavius Octavius IV** as script supervisor, to maintain the operational continuity of the novel-writing process itself. Octavius IV's assistant is Elijah's sister (name open) — the boy's family is on the staff of his own vision.

I eventually fire Octavius IV: the assistant was doing everything, and Octavius himself is unreliable. The paradox is the point — I am unreliable (that is why I hired a script supervisor in the first place), the supervisor is unreliable, and the only reliable continuity worker is a child's sister, inside the child's vision. An absurd loop: the continuity instrument fails, in a series about failure.

The Chronotopium itself appears in vol. 2 only as inklings — premonitions inside Elijah's visions. The reader feels something coming without being shown it.

## 8. The theories, per novel

Each novel carries its own set of theories, put in the characters' mouths:

- **Vol. 1:** **Historiotherapeusis** — historical therapy, Kropotkin's invention; its theory is stated in the 2006 "Final Analysis: A theory of Historical Therapy using the Concept of Habitance." **Habitance** is Evelyn's thesis concept, and in the period documents it stands alone: "the way in which beings inhabit time and space… an inhabitor of habit, a being of habit" (alias: the Lowland Sickness). The triad the concept later grew — migrance / habitance / survivance — is a later development, not a period one. The volume's premise, in the period text's own words: "the historical cause of Montreal's specific brand of morose anxiety."
- **Vol. 2:** Historiomics (history as epigenetic inscription; the **historiome** — an immutable, append-only event log, a store of Points-in-Time whose deltas are historiotopes — and the antihistoriome, its shadow), signal science, noise field theory. Plus: **historiophany** (below), historionauts, historiotomes (blockages), historiopathies, historiotherapeusis.
- **Vol. 3:** workspace theory — the **Δ-workspace theory**, the **historiotopia** (sometimes historiotope). Formalized, almost mathematical, becomes code. The Δ-workspace tracks deltas — indicators of change; every change reflected in the research and the work; studying change and trend lines to optimize workflow and workspace management. The analogy: as AI needs a world model to act in the world, an operation needs a model of its workspace to work in it. And **Chronoplasticity**: the Chronotopium's time is plastic — it deforms to absorb deviation. That is the mechanism by which risk is laundered out of choice (§9).

**Historiophany.** The word is mine, and the operation that makes it is mine: historiophany is my secularization of the religious-mystical concept of Christophany — a theophany secularized *down* to a historiophany. It is history revealed as lived experience, through crisis (the physio-image is its form). Its movement: consciousness reaches a breaking point, a collapse; a decision point is reached, diverging into different directions; you choose a direction and flow into it — the directionality of it. It was always there, from the first volume onward: the characters underwent it without realizing they were theorizing, until Panikkar named it (§7). The aha-interruption is a historiophany happening to a reader (§10).

My note on the workspace theory: it is nearly my entire life's work — thirty years. Its origin: a family member's workshop, glimpsed at seven, eight, nine, ten years old — the mess of tools everywhere, the oscilloscope, nails hammered into sculptures. The workspace I wasn't supposed to enter; the mess I made things from anyway.

## 9. The Chronotopium

A utopia of synchrony — a frictionless planet, everything smooth — but with a pulse, not a flatline: **"Babel up and Babel down,"** the rhythm of rising and falling; deliberate scale-downs of operations so things can rest; the seasonal creature; cycles superimposed (nature, hormones, and others). In the cosmology of versions (§4), the pulse is interference: versions rising and falling against one another. A flatline would be every version phase-locked into one — which is to say, the flaw, achieved.

Archivillus holds not just the archive and the errors but the **solutions** to every problem. Anyone, anywhere on Earth, opens an invisible portal and simply talks to it — and it patches the bugs. The bug-patcher of civilization.

Archivillus is the savior of the third novel, and the reason is structural: he catalogs, documents, logs, and archives all the errors — and so in the end it gets amended, it gets fixed. The "good story time ending," the fairy-tale ending: Archivillus and the Chronotopium come and save everything. The collector of omissions turns out to be the repairman.

**The fatal flaw (discovered 2026-10-17).** None of that is revised — and it cannot stand alone, because I discovered the flaw in it. The argument, part of it from a discussion of Catholic theology: God judges a universe with creatures capable of authentic love — which inherently carries the risk of genuine rejection and moral failure — a greater good than a universe of programmed automata who cannot sin but cannot truly love. Remove the risks of rejection and failure, and you remove the freedom of choice, which negates the existence of love. The Chronotopium is about as holy as anything humans have ever created; it very much is the City of God, of the Kingdom — and its whole theology rests on freedom and love. A chronological utopia of perfect synchrony has laundered the risk out of every choice (chronoplasticity is the mechanism, §8). So there will be no love in the Chronotopium — no authentic love — and it must also be an abject failure: the third failure, by completion (§2).

**The annexes — two endings.** The novel proper is the perfect synchrony, the chronological utopia, with the savior ending above, unrevised. Alongside it stand the design concepts I wrote for Archivillus, as annexes — the other ending, in two designs. The first design (my Archivillus text, © 2025): a supermassive archive *of* failures, errors, threats, conflicts and crises, whose story is its own metafailure — corrosion by a consortium profiting from erasure, testimonies overwritten — resolving into the archive as a constellation of living, contested memory practices. It fails by **corrosion**: freedom is everywhere in it; harm and love are both possible. The second annex, *The Archivillus Fragments*, is another version of the concept, a collage of shards from the trilogy's cast. The two designs are the two terms of the same argument: corrosion, where everything can go wrong and love is possible; completion, where nothing can go wrong and love is not. (The exact annex structure is still settling; the two-endings doctrine is settled.)

Note on the word: **Chronotopium** (not "Chronotopian") — a chronological utopia, but Chronos-time, non-linear. The novel is non-linear without being hypertextual: no links, no branching paths. The method is ideation — the simulation running in the mind. (I wanted to write my own Choose Your Own Adventure novels as a child; this is the better version I found: no paths to choose, because the whole thing is simulated at once.)

The post-archival condition (my word): the entire record of human civilization and the arts — millions of years of it — totally modeled, like a foundation model but more advanced, formalized and understood completely by machines. No physical archive remains; everything simulated in the virtual machine. (My real-world POST-ARCHIVAL work pulls the other way — dismantling the archive. Same word, opposite horizons; the novels run the experiment to the end.)

Vol. 3's systems: **HistoriOS** (the Historiosphere Engine) — Historionautics, Historiotronics, Chronotronics — and the **Anti-OS**. Not always clearly distinguished; the ambiguity is deliberate. From my 2015 notes on the Anti-OS (codenamed "Antioch"), the older machinery survives: Points-in-Time and Restoration Points, the B:Drive, TimeMarks, the History-Machine — navigation, in those notes, through Crackland.

## 10. The reader's contract

Nobody knows what time it is — not the characters, not the archivists, not the reader. The disorientation is the method. In my phrase: **"the historical self-presentation of one's history"** — the work gradually presents its own history to you, the way an archive yields itself only to the patient. The reader does exactly what the archivists do.

On my voice: the dated, typed notes are the voice — confessional, funny, profane, self-lacerating, typos left standing ("spit-filled sputterings of a delusional man"). The polished overviews are machine summaries of that material. The voice to write toward is the dated notes.

The narrator is unreliable on purpose, and the unreliability is autobiographical: I have poor verbal memory and cognitive disabilities, and the fictionalized author carries them too — a man who compensates for his disabilities by writing novels. Which is, I note, pretty true. I am a realist, a naturalist, in the line of Émile Zola's essay *The Experimental Novel* — except Zola wasn't really writing an experimental novel, not the way Burroughs was, and not the way this is. Zola's experiment was methodological (the novelist as scientist, after Claude Bernard); this one is ontological: the novel as an experiment conducted on itself.

The continuity thread is a Kierkegaardian indirect method — Kierkegaard's theory of indirect communication. The metafiction must stay followable — there has to be a thread, or it becomes hard to read — but the vaporia, the aporia, the absurdity and paradox are intentional: the reader is meant to dissociate, healthily, and have a subjective realization about their own life. Inside that method sits one moment I once staged on its own: the book written to *not* be read — the reader has a realization while reading, puts the book down, and goes on with their life. That interruption is a historiophany happening to a reader (§8). But it is one moment within indirect communication, not the whole of it: I want people to read, and to enjoy reading. The novel is a bridge between the reader and the writer, a chamber where they can be together without knowing each other — a kind of union, the way a historiophany connects you with a historical person. The encounter is the point.

## 13. World building, character building, story building

**The story bible as NOUS.** My own story bible runs thousands of pages longer than my novel — the novel stands at ~112–120 pages, full of holes and mistakes. The bible is the ideal, palatial NOUS; the novels are the fallen attempt. In the terms of this series: the story bible is the work's Archivillus — complete, logged, amended — and the physical manuscript is the error-filled record he feeds on. The continuity instrument one level up from the fiction.

**Abstract characterology.** My worked-out theory — and, as I stated it on 2026-10-17, the generation system of §4 *is* the theory, inside the novelistic phenomenology. Its heart is the **problem-space**: character problems are the heart of creation, all the way back down — physics itself is a problematic engine, an engine of problems; there is an algebra and a topology to space and time. The problem-space is generative: it generates all the other forms in the novel and in the phenomenology, and then my inner life. The system works by **proximity**: the most proximate character is me, the author; next, the characters transmuted from my own experience and history — Alphonse first among them; transmutation, almost transubstantiation, is the process. It is most costly to go back to the source — the stream of characterology runs outward, and the return upstream is the expensive movement. Characters are *character agents* — they have agency, especially by the third novel (§7). Every social species, especially social mammals, has to have an abstract characterology — a sort of theory of mind, but for the novelist: the system by which invented people become agents. When I started *The History-Project* the ambition was purist and absolutist: to write *pure characters* — invented people who don't exist, true fiction — as a formalist of the art of the novel. The full doctrine lives in the Abstract Characterology theory document; this bible carries its place inside the novels and refers there for the detail.

**Vol. 1's period ground.** The first version of this bible was thin on vol. 1 because its main surviving body of text was not yet in the room. What the audit established: the surviving *digital* body of the novel is the dated document of September 2003–December 2004 — not the typewritten manuscript, which is a paper object (over 100 pages written, under 100 surviving, much lost to water damage). The period stratum gives the volume two complete tables of contents; the government-commission plot (Soutine hired by the Canadian Government, a powerful Ministry of Canadian Heritage; the government wants the project shut down, and Soutine persists); the Manual, and "A Season in The Life of a Manual"; and the project's stated purpose, in the January 2004 notes: "to come up with a North American, French-Canadian characterology, of the Habitant and his or her family, friends, acquaintances" — with Kierkegaard in the individual characterology, each character "an individual in charge of an individual destiny." The characterology of this whole work is rooted here, in vol. 1, in 2004. The period notes also carry the political-prisoner allegory key. And Crackland — written at the same time (2000–2004), essentially fused with the novel in one master work — is the annex document of this volume, inside *The History-Project*'s own folder: not a second bible beside this one.

**Biblical typology.** As the trilogy moves on, I feed developed ideas back into earlier material, planting prefigurations: type and anti-type, one presaging the other, mirror images in some symbolic way. Types are load-bearing.

**Life world.** Much of the material is my life world, phenomenologically: the way the novel appears in my mind, the ideas arriving, and how I follow them down tangents, down rabbit holes. World building, story building — and the life world underneath them both.
