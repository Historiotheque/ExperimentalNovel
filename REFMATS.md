# REFMATS — The Revolt of Fiction

*An illuminated bibliography. Not just references: each entry carries the author's interpretation — how the reference is understood, and how it lives (or pointedly does not live) in the work.*

*The doctrine: **winks, not theft.** A wink is just a reference — interpreted properly, attributed fully, and left as a wink. The work aims to be as unique and original as possible; nothing here is taken, only acknowledged.*

*Format: Chicago-style citation, then the illumination.*

## Burroughs, William S. — the cut-up method

Burroughs, William S., and Brion Gysin. *The Third Mind*. New York: Viking Press, 1978.

**Illumination.** Life is cut up. It is non-linear — you can cut from one scene to the other. The author learned the method the way Burroughs taught it: listening to recordings of Burroughs *talking about it*, not just reading the results. The cut-up is therefore understood as a worldview before it is a technique — the page cut the way life cuts. Interpreted, attributed, never imitated. (The F1 text-engine family descends from this entry.)

## Gysin, Brion — co-originator of the cut-up

**Illumination.** The cut-up came from Gysin; Burroughs made it famous. The lineage matters: methods have authors, and the authors get named. (Cf. the constitution's Article 6 — the visible discipline.)

## Recut (film) — the re-edit as a form

**Illumination.** A recut takes existing footage and re-edits it into a new work — the recut trailer that turns a horror film into a romantic comedy. The term belongs here because the author's *tonal cinema* works the same way with scenes: cut from one to the other, re-edit reality. The recut is the cut-up's cinematic sibling.

## Acker, Kathy — the recut as literary method

Acker, Kathy. *Blood and Guts in High School*. New York: Grove Press, 1984. — *Great Expectations*. New York: Grove Press, 1983. — *Don Quixote*. New York: Grove Press, 1986.

**Illumination.** Acker's method was "plagiarism" as praxis: rewriting canonical texts into her own novels — "I use other texts, that's how I write"; "I don't like the idea of originality." Received first as theft and scandal, reframed by her as feminist guerrilla warfare, later canonized. Kept here as **contrast**: this is not the author's method. Where Acker stole, the author winks. The entry stays because the difference is instructive — the avant-garde has always treated theft-as-method as legitimate lineage (Burroughs got the cut-up from Gysin; Acker got her novels from everybody), and knowing exactly where you stand in that lineage is part of the work.

## Tonal cinema / the Exhibition — the author's own

**Illumination.** Sixteen novels and novellas and one novel, built on the concept of a *tonal cinema*: a cinema, but tonal — because it was poetry first. "Tonal cinema" was what the author called his poetry in the beginning, before it became *the Exhibition*. The Exhibition, or tonal cinema, names the methodology and the style. *(To be developed — this is a whole other thing, and it gets its own folders.)*

## Interzone — the homage

**Illumination.** Verified 2026-09-26: the Tangier International Zone was real (1924–1956); Burroughs lived in Tangier and told Ginsberg that *Naked Lunch*'s Interzone was a stand-in for it. The staging buffer for this very project is named after it — a wink, in the doctrine's sense. (See the Interzone file's protocol.)
